# Introduction to Greek Verbs

## 1. One Word, a Whole Sentence

A single Greek verb form carries a great deal of information at once — who is acting, how many of them, when, in what manner, and whether they act or are acted upon. All of it sits in one word, in the stem and the ending:

- παιδεύομεν *paideúomen* = "we are educating" (who: *we*; when/how: *ongoing present*; voice: *active*)
- ἐπαιδεύθης *epaideúthēs* = "you were educated" (*you*, *past, single event*, *passive*)

Every finite verb form encodes five things: **person**, **number**, **tense/aspect**, **voice**, and **mood**.

## 2. The Five Components

### Person and Number

| | Singular | Plural |
|---|---|---|
| 1st | I | we |
| 2nd | you (sg.) | you (pl.) |
| 3rd | he/she/it | they |

Because the ending shows the person, subject pronouns are usually omitted: λέγω *légō* by itself means "I speak." (There is also a rare dual for pairs.)

### Tense — Really Aspect

Greek "tenses" say less about *when* than about *how the action unfolds* (its **aspect**):

| Tense | Aspect + time | Force |
|---|---|---|
| Present | ongoing, now | "I am loosening" |
| Imperfect | ongoing, past | "I was loosening" |
| Future | later | "I will loosen" |
| Aorist | single event, past (in the indicative) | "I loosened" |
| Perfect | completed, with present result | "I have loosened" |
| Pluperfect | completed, with past result | "I had loosened" |

Outside the indicative mood (e.g., in infinitives and commands), aorist vs. present marks *only* aspect — single event vs. ongoing — not past time. This idea takes getting used to, and the lessons will return to it often.

### Voice

- **Active**: the subject acts — λύω *lýō* "I loosen"
- **Middle**: the subject acts on/for itself or with self-involvement — λύομαι *lýomai* "I ransom (get someone freed for myself)"
- **Passive**: the subject is acted upon — λύομαι *lýomai* "I am being loosened"

Middle and passive share their forms in most tenses; context decides. Some verbs — called **deponents** — have only middle/passive forms but active meanings: ἔρχομαι *érchomai* "I come", βούλομαι *boúlomai* "I want".

### Mood

| Mood | Signals | Example | Transliteration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Indicative | statement of fact | λύει "he loosens" | lýei |
| Subjunctive | possibility, purpose, exhortation | ἵνα λύῃ "so that he may loosen" | hína lýēi |
| Optative | wish, remote possibility | λύοι "may he loosen!" | lýoi |
| Imperative | command | λῦε "loosen!" | lŷe |

Two further verb forms behave like nouns and adjectives: the **infinitive** (λύειν *lýein* "to loosen") and the **participle** (λύων *lýōn* "loosening"), both of which Greek uses with great freedom, often building whole subordinate clauses around a single participle.

## 3. Anatomy of a Verb Form

A typical form assembles like this:

**(augment) + stem + tense marker + ending**

ἐ-παίδευ-σα-μεν *e-paídeu-sa-men* "we educated": augment ἐ- *e-* (past time), stem παιδευ- *paideu-*, aorist marker -σα- *-sa-*, ending -μεν *-men* (1st plural).

## 4. Principal Parts

Because stems change across tenses, dictionaries list **six principal parts** for each verb, from which every form can be built. For παιδεύω *paideúō* "educate":

1. παιδεύω *paideúō* (present)
2. παιδεύσω *paideúsō* (future)
3. ἐπαίδευσα *epaídeusa* (aorist active)
4. πεπαίδευκα *pepaídeuka* (perfect active)
5. πεπαίδευμαι *pepaídeumai* (perfect middle/passive)
6. ἐπαιδεύθην *epaideúthēn* (aorist passive)

Regular verbs like this are predictable; frequent irregular verbs must be memorized — and the frequency-based lessons in this book prioritize exactly those.

## 5. A Note on Dialect and Period

Your reading passages may come from texts written centuries apart, so some forms will not match the paradigm. Three shifts affect the verb system in particular, and they are worth knowing before you start:

- **The optative fades.** Common and supple in Homer and Classical Attic, it declines sharply in the Hellenistic period and is nearly gone from the New Testament, surviving mostly in the fixed phrase μὴ γένοιτο *mḕ génoito* "may it not be so."
- **The perfect merges with the aorist.** Classical Greek keeps them distinct — the aorist reports an event, the perfect a state that still holds. In Koine that distinction erodes and the perfect drifts toward being a plain past tense.
- **The infinitive gives way to ἵνα *hína* clauses**, the beginning of a change that Modern Greek completed.

## Summary

- One Greek verb form = person + number + tense/aspect + voice + mood.
- Aspect (ongoing vs. single event vs. completed-with-result) matters more than time.
- Learn verbs by their principal parts; the endings then follow regular patterns.
